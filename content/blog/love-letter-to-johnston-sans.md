# A love letter to Johnston Sans

The first time I actually saw Johnston Sans, rather than just reading it, I was on a platform at Bethnal Green waiting for a Central line train that was not coming. I had been reading this typeface for years without looking at it. That is the whole trick of it, and I did not understand the trick until I stopped.

Most Londoners do not know they read Johnston every day. It is on the front of every bus, the side of every Tube carriage, every wayfinding sign from Heathrow to Upminster. A typeface that teaches itself to disappear is the hardest kind to design, and Johnston does it beautifully.

---

## The man who made it

Edward Johnston was born in 1872 and came to lettering the long way round. He trained briefly as a doctor, abandoned medicine, and in his mid twenties began teaching himself calligraphy from medieval manuscripts at the British Museum. He started teaching at the Central School of Arts and Crafts, and in 1906 he published *Writing & Illuminating, & Lettering*, a book that taught a generation how to hold a broad-nibbed pen and what to do with it. It is still in print.

He was a mystic in a very Arts and Crafts way. He believed letterforms were spiritual objects, that good lettering was a kind of moral practice, that the hand and the eye and the tool were not separable from each other or from the work they produced. He taught Eric Gill, who later designed Gill Sans as a commercial reinterpretation of his teacher's work. We will come back to that.

In 1913, Frank Pick, who would later become managing director of London Transport, went looking for someone to design a typeface for the Underground. Pick's brief was ambitious: something dignified and elegant, but also unmistakably modern. Something that could unify a chaotic network of private railways into a single legible system.

Johnston delivered the finished typeface in 1916, cut in metal by Caslon that same year. He also designed the Underground's roundel, the red circle with the blue bar, in 1919. Both are still in use. Both are older than almost every person reading them.

---

## The brief behind the typeface

This is the part I find most quietly radical about the whole thing.

Pick and Johnston were not designing a typeface in isolation. They were designing a system. Signage, colour, symbol, poster, station, map: all of it held together by a consistent typographic voice and a consistent graphic mark. This was design systems thinking before anyone had the language for it. A hundred years before Figma files and design tokens, two men were working out that a public service only feels like one service if it sounds like one service on every surface.

The typeface had to carry that. It had to work large on enamel signs, small on ticket stubs, legible on moving buses, calm on underground platforms where everyone was in a hurry. Johnston solved it with a geometric humanist sans: geometric enough to feel modern, humanist enough to remain warm, loosely proportioned so it never felt mechanical.

He drew it before Futura existed. Paul Renner would not release Futura until 1927. Johnston got to the idea of a geometric sans more than a decade earlier, from a different starting point: not modernist zeal, but a calligrapher's sense of form.

---

## The diamond tittle

If you want to know Johnston at a glance, look at the lowercase i and the lowercase j.

The dot is not a dot. It is a diamond. A small, perfect rhombus sitting above the stem, tilted on its axis, catching the eye without meaning to. Any serious calligrapher of Johnston's generation would have recognised it as a scribe's mark, the shape a broad-nibbed pen naturally makes when it touches paper at an angle and lifts off. Johnston took that scribe's accident and froze it into the typeface as a signature.

You cannot see a diamond tittle and think you are reading anything else. It is the most quietly confident piece of design in the whole face.

## The perfect circles

The O, the C, and the G are drawn as near-perfect circles. Not the slightly-squashed ovals you get in most humanist sans. Actual geometric circles. They give the typeface its calm and its rhythm. A Johnston word sits on a line of text like a row of evenly spaced stones. There is a pulse to it.

## The splayed M

The capital M has diagonals that reach all the way to the full width of the letter at the top. It is a wide, planted letter, a little architectural. It does not lean in on itself the way Gill Sans's M does. It stands there.

## The letter-spacing

Most sans-serifs from the early twentieth century were spaced tight. Johnston is spaced loose. Generous. Almost airy. On a sign seen from across a platform this reads as calm: the letters are not shouting, they are waiting for you to notice them. Up close it reads as confidence. Johnston is a typeface that was not worried about fitting.

---

## Johnston and Gill

This needs saying and then parking.

Eric Gill studied under Johnston at the Central School. When Monotype asked Gill for a sans-serif, he drew on everything he had learned from his teacher and produced Gill Sans, released in 1928. Gill Sans went commercial; Johnston's typeface stayed exclusive to London Transport. Gill Sans became one of the most widely used typefaces of the twentieth century. Johnston became the private voice of one city's transport network.

The two share DNA. But they are not the same. Gill's letters are a touch more mannered, narrower, more self-consciously British. Johnston's are more serene, more geometrically pure, less interested in being admired. If Gill Sans is a typeface that wants you to notice it is well drawn, Johnston is a typeface that wants you to notice the train is arriving in two minutes.

---

## A hundred years of service

The typeface has been redrawn twice since 1916, both times with care.

In 1979 Eiichi Kono at Banks & Miles produced **New Johnston**, adapting the original for phototypesetting and expanding it into a proper family of weights. This is the version most Londoners grew up reading.

In 2016, for the typeface's centenary, Monotype redrew it again as **Johnston100**, a project led by Jim Wasco. Johnston100 rebuilt the typeface for digital use: more weights, refined proportions, new hairline and thin cuts, a full OpenType feature set. It is the version currently on every TfL surface, from the Elizabeth line to bus blinds to the TfL app.

The through-line from 1916 to now is unbroken. The Johnston you read on a Victoria line platform today descends directly from the lead type Caslon cut during the First World War. No other working typeface has that provenance in active public service.

---

## Why it still works

Johnston works because it was never trying to be fashionable. Pick asked for something unmistakably modern, and Johnston delivered, but he built it out of shapes a calligrapher would have recognised in a twelfth-century manuscript: round bowls, honest proportions, a pen's relationship to paper. The modernity is in the restraint, not the novelty.

Fashions in sans-serif design have come and gone. Grotesque, geometric, neo-grotesque, humanist again, variable, monolinear minimalism. Johnston has outlasted all of them by not participating. It says London, it says calm, and it says the train is coming. Those are the only three things it has ever needed to say.

---

## Where to find it

Johnston proper remains licensed exclusively to Transport for London. You cannot buy it. You can, however, get close.

**ITC Johnston**, released in 1999 by Richard Dawson and David Farey, is the commercial digitisation closest to the original. It is the one to reach for if you want Johnston's shapes on a page.

**P22 Underground** is another licensed digitisation, a little more expressive, a little more display-oriented.

**Hammersmith One**, free on Google Fonts, is a loose Johnston-inspired single-weight sans. Not a revival, not a substitute, but a related shape with a similar feeling.

For the actual thing, the only way to read it is to get on the Tube.

---

I keep coming back to Johnston because it is the typeface that taught me what invisibility means in design. Not blandness. Not neutrality. Something harder: the quality of having been made with so much attention that the attention stops registering as attention at all.

Every time I am back in London I read Johnston on about forty surfaces before lunch, and I do not notice most of them. That is the work. Edward Johnston drew a typeface in 1916 to get strangers through a confusing city, and it is still doing that job, on enamel and vinyl and pixel, more than a century later.

I do not think you can say that about anything else.
