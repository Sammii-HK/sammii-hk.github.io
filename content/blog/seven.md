Press **Step the hours** on [labs.sammii.dev/charts/seven](https://labs.sammii.dev/charts/seven/) and a circle of seven planets starts lighting up one at a time, round and round. In the middle it says Saturday, hour 1, ruled by Saturn. Then hour 2, ruled by Jupiter. Then Mars, the Sun, Venus, Mercury, the Moon, and back to Saturn.

Nothing much seems to happen for a while. Then, 24 hours in, the centre switches to Sunday, and a line is drawn from Saturn to the Sun. A day later another line goes from the Sun to the Moon. By the end of the week the lines have drawn a seven-pointed star, and the order it visits the planets in is the order of the days of the week.

## Seven by arithmetic

Seven is the number cultures reach for when they want a set to feel complete. Heavens, chakras, notes, colours, wonders, sins. Most of those are seven by choice. The week is the one that is seven by arithmetic, and it is the one you use every day.

The days are named for the seven classical planets, the bodies that visibly move against the fixed stars: Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, the Sun, Venus, Mercury and the Moon. But the week does not go in that order. Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday is Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn. So where did that order come from?

From the hours. Put the planets in the ancients' order, slowest to fastest across the sky. (This is the Chaldean order. It is by apparent speed against the stars, which the ancients read as distance: Saturn slowest and outermost, the Moon fastest and nearest.) Give each hour of the day to the next planet in that order, round and round, and name each day after the planet that rules its first hour.

A day has 24 hours, and 24 mod 7 = 3. So the planet ruling the first hour of each new day is three places on from the one before. Saturday starts with Saturn. Three on is the Sun. Three on from that is the Moon, then Mars, then Mercury, then Jupiter, then Venus. That is the week. Drawn on the circle, a step of three round seven points is the {7/3} heptagram, the star the chart draws.

This is not a modern reconstruction. Cassius Dio, writing around 230 CE, gives exactly this account in his Roman History (37.18 to 19): the Egyptians assigning each hour to a planet in the order of their spheres, and the day taking the name of its first hour.

## The controls

There are three. **Step the hours** plays the week an hour at a time and turns into **Pause** while it runs. **Reset** takes you back to the first hour of Saturday. And the **Hour of the week** slider, 1 to 168, lets you drag to any hour, which is the quickest way to see the star: pull it to the end and all seven lines are there.

As each day starts, its planet gets the day's short name beside it (Sun, Mon, Tue and so on), and a line under the controls tells you where the current day's name came from in the languages that kept the gods: Tiw's day and mardi for Mars, Woden's day and mercredi for Mercury, Thor's day and jeudi for Jupiter, Frigg's day and vendredi for Venus. The chart says it plainly: the names are the same seven gods in every European language that kept them.

## Seven, in columns

Under the star is a table of sevens, one row each, so each column reads down: Newton's spectrum, the musical scale, the week, its planets, the chakras, and the seven spheres of A Journey Through Light, my MA installation.

The note under the table is the most important sentence on the page: the rows are not claimed to correspond, only to count. Two pairs genuinely do correspond. The week and its planets correspond mechanically, as above. And the spectrum and the scale correspond historically, because of Newton.

Newton saw the spectrum as a continuum. He first divided it into five colours, then added orange and indigo so that light would share a number with the seven notes of the musical scale. He divides the spectrum in proportion to the intervals of the octave in Opticks, Book I, in 1704. The rainbow has seven colours because Newton wanted it to, and indigo is there to make the count.

The rest are seven because seven had already become the number you reach for.

## Seven, counted

The last section is a short list:

- **7 ± 2**: the items a person can hold in working memory, in Miller's famous estimate from 1956. The footer adds that later work puts the honest figure nearer four.
- **1 in 10**: people who name seven as their favourite number in Alex Bellos's survey of 44,000 people, the most popular answer. His reason is lovely: of the first ten numbers, seven is the only one you can neither multiply nor divide within the group. It feels unique, so it feels complete.
- Seven heavens in Judaism and Islam, with God above the seventh; seven circuits of the Kaaba; seven wedding blessings.
- Seven higher worlds and seven underworlds in Hinduism; seven horses pulling the sun god's chariot; seven gods of fortune in Japan.
- Seven planetary spheres the soul descends through in the Hermetica, which is where the week, the metals and the days all inherit the count.

## What it does not claim

The chart explains why the week is in that order. It does not explain why there are 24 hours in a day, and it does not argue that any of this is true about the planets. It is an account of how a naming system worked.

The table counts; it does not connect. Only the two pairs above are claimed to correspond.

The cultural sevens are from one person's research for an art installation, not a survey of world religions, and they are short entries rather than the full story of each. And Miller's 7 ± 2 is on the list because it is famous, with the correction attached.
