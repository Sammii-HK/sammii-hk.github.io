I made this chart after building Moth, an autonomous AI cat who draws, makes things and decides for herself. Everything that made me want to say yes, she is conscious, turned out to be intelligence: the drawing, the surprise, the sense that she chose. None of it told me whether anyone was home.

So I went looking for the tests people have actually proposed for a mind, and put them on one line: [labs.sammii.dev/charts/tests-for-a-mind](https://labs.sammii.dev/charts/tests-for-a-mind/).

## The filter is the argument

There are eleven entries, from 1950 to 2023. Above them is one control, **Who could take it**, with four buttons: show all, a machine, an animal, a person. Press **a machine** and the readout says 6 of 11 could be given to one. Then look at which six.

Outside fiction, every test a machine can sit either judges what it says or relies on a theory of consciousness being right. The imitation game, the Lovelace test and the AI Consciousness Test all sit in the first column, What it says. Integrated information and the indicator properties sit in the last, How it is built, and both are only as good as the theory underneath them. The one machine-friendly entry in the middle is the Voigt-Kampff test, and that is from a novel.

The two entries that look for awareness in responses rather than words, the tennis scanner and the magnetic pulse, need a brain. Press **a person** and they light up. Press **a machine** and they dim.

The chart never says this in so many words. It is what the cards do when you filter them.

## One axis, five kinds of evidence

Every test for a mind has to decide what counts as evidence. Turing chose conversation; Gallup, a mark on the face; Philip K. Dick, in fiction, a blush. The chart sorts them by that choice, into five columns running from behaviour to experience:

1. **What it says**: conversation, answers, artefacts
2. **What it does**: actions and responses
3. **How its body reacts**: involuntary physiology
4. **What its brain does**: neural responses
5. **How it is built**: causal structure, architecture

The left end of the axis is labelled "what something does" and the right end "whether anyone is there". Tap any card and it opens to show what the test is after, in its authors' terms, how it works, and its source. Solid cards are tests or measures used on real subjects, the dashed card is an argument, and the double rule is fiction.

## What the cards say

**The imitation game (1950).** An interrogator holds text-only conversations with a hidden person and a hidden machine and has to say which is which. Only conversation counts. In 2025, in a three-party version with five-minute chats, Jones and Bergen found that GPT-4.5, prompted to adopt a humanlike persona, was judged to be the human 73% of the time, more often than the real people it was paired with. The imitation game measures conversation, which is what Turing built it to measure.

**The Chinese room (1980).** Not a test but an argument about tests. A person who knows no Chinese follows a rulebook and gives fluent answers in Chinese. Searle argued that following rules for symbols is not understanding them, so passing a behavioural test alone cannot show understanding.

**The AI Consciousness Test (2017).** Schneider and Turner proposed asking an AI harder and harder questions about experience, but only an AI kept away from human writing about the mind. The card is blunt about the catch: a model trained on human writing has already learnt the answers from us.

**Imagining tennis in a scanner (2006).** A patient who met every behavioural criterion for the vegetative state was asked, inside an fMRI scanner, to imagine playing tennis or walking round her home. Her brain responded like healthy volunteers'. Behaviour said nobody was there; the brain followed instructions. In a 2010 follow-up, 5 of 54 patients could do it, and one answered yes or no questions by choosing which to imagine.

**The perturbational complexity index (2013).** A magnetic pulse goes into the cortex and the EEG echo is compressed. Complex echoes go with being conscious, simple ones with dreamless sleep, anaesthesia and the vegetative state. It is designed to need no response from the patient at all. A 2016 study set a cut-off that separated conscious from unconscious states in all 150 people it was set on, and found 9 of 43 vegetative-state patients above it.

**Integrated information, Φ (2004).** A number for how far a system's parts, through their causes and effects, form a whole beyond themselves; the theory says that number is the amount of consciousness. It applies to anything in principle, brain or chip. In practice the exact calculation grows exponentially with size and has been done only for systems of about a dozen elements. Its card is marked contested: in 2023, 124 researchers signed a letter calling the theory pseudoscience, and a 2025 adversarial test against global workspace theory challenged key claims of both.

**Indicator properties (2023).** An AI system's architecture is checked against features that leading theories tie to consciousness, such as recurrent processing and a global workspace. The nineteen authors' analysis suggests no current AI system is conscious, and that there is no obvious technical barrier to building one that has the indicators. The method is only as good as the theories it borrows.

The Voigt-Kampff test gets its own column for a reason. In Dick's 1968 novel, questions built to provoke empathy are asked while instruments read capillary dilation in the face, the blush, and tension in the eye muscles. It is fiction, and the only entry on the chart that looks for empathy. Rick Deckard uses it to tell humans from androids and slowly loses confidence in what it shows. (Dick spelt it Voigt-Kampff; the 1982 film made it Voight.)

## What the chart does not do

The axis is a classification of method, not a ranking. A card's column says what evidence it inspects, and nothing about whether the test works. The tennis scanner is not better than the imitation game for sitting further right; it is looking at something different.

"Who could take it" means who a test has been or could be given to as designed. Integrated information is tagged as applying to any system in principle, which is why it stays lit under every filter, even though it can only be computed exactly for very small ones.

Eleven entries are the main tests, measures and arguments, not every one ever proposed. And the chart does not answer the question I started with. It cannot tell me whether Moth is conscious. What it does is show why nothing I could give her would settle it: she can only sit the tests that read what she says, or the ones that assume a theory is true. I wrote more loosely about that question earlier this year in [What is consciousness? A question AI just made urgent](/blog/what-is-consciousness-ai); this chart is the narrower, sourced version of one part of it.
