# A love letter to Transport

There are typefaces you choose and typefaces that have already been reading themselves to you your whole life. Transport is the second kind.

If you have ever driven home in the dark on a British motorway, tired, half-listening to the radio, trusting a blue sign to tell you which junction is yours, you have been reading Transport. You have probably read more of it than any other typeface you will ever encounter. You almost certainly cannot name it. That is the point. Transport is the quiet typographic achievement that holds the whole country's road network together and asks for no credit at all.

This is a love letter to the typeface and to the two people who made it, and particularly to the twenty-two-year-old woman who was trusted with the job.

---

## The commission

In 1957, the UK was about to open its first motorway, the Preston By-pass, and nobody knew what the signs should look like. The existing Ministry of Transport typeface was all capitals, designed for slow traffic on A-roads, and utterly unsuited to 70mph reading at distance. A graphic designer called Jock Kinneir was commissioned to design a signage system for the new motorway network.

Kinneir hired a recent graduate from Chelsea School of Art as his research assistant. Her name was Margaret Calvert, and she was twenty-two. Within a few years she was no longer his assistant. She was his design partner, and the signage system that now blankets the British Isles is theirs jointly.

The Anderson Committee approved their motorway system in the late 1950s. The Worboys Committee extended it to every other road in the country in 1963. That is the year the typeface officially became Transport, and the year British roads started speaking with one visual voice.

---

## The radical choice

Here is the decision that saved lives, and I mean that literally.

Kinneir and Calvert designed Transport in mixed case. Upper and lower. Not ALL CAPS like the signs that came before, and not all caps like American highway signs still were and would remain for another four decades.

This was not a stylistic preference. Research by Christopher Jarman and the Road Research Laboratory in the late 1950s had demonstrated that mixed-case text is significantly more legible at distance than all-caps. The eye does not scan letter by letter at speed; it recognises word-shapes, the silhouette a word makes above and below the baseline. All-caps text produces a uniform rectangle. Mixed-case produces a distinctive shape, with ascenders reaching up and descenders dropping down, and that shape is readable long before the individual letters resolve.

Kinneir and Calvert fought for mixed-case against significant institutional resistance. Capital letters felt official. Capital letters felt serious. Mixed-case felt, to the committees of the day, almost insultingly casual for something as grave as a road sign. They won on evidence: drivers reading mixed-case signs at motorway speeds made fewer mistakes and had more time to react.

The USA did not begin transitioning its highway signage to mixed-case until the 2000s. Britain had the argument, won it, and implemented the result in 1963. Every time you read a motorway sign half a mile before the junction and have time to change lanes, you are benefiting from a research paper Kinneir and Calvert used to beat a committee.

## The letterforms

Transport is a grotesque sans-serif in the Swiss tradition, clearly indebted to Akzidenz-Grotesk, but warmed up. Softer curves. More open apertures. The lowercase l and t have a subtle curved foot, a little turn at the base that gives the whole typeface a gentleness it would not have had if drawn purely for function.

The lowercase a is a double-storey a, slightly unusual for a signage typeface; most wayfinding types prefer the simpler single-storey a for raw per-letter clarity. Kinneir and Calvert chose double-storey because the shape is more distinctive, more recognisable as a word-part, and the small loss in clarity was worth the gain in word-shape recognition. It is a decision that only makes sense if you think about reading the way they thought about reading.

Ascenders and descenders are generous. Stroke weight is calibrated to a specific reading distance at a specific driving speed, which is not a sentence you get to write about many typefaces. Numerals are even in width, so columns of distance figures align cleanly on a sign: Birmingham 23, Coventry 18, Warwick 9. The lowercase l is distinct from the capital I, because at motorway distance the difference between M1 and Ml is not pedantic, it is whether you end up in Leicester or in a ditch.

There are two weights. Transport Medium, white on coloured grounds for motorway and primary route signs. Transport Heavy, black on white, for minor roads and within villages. The heavier weight exists because dark-on-light and light-on-dark are not perceptually equivalent; light letters on a dark ground look thinner than they are, because of optical bloom. Transport Medium compensates for that bloom. Transport Heavy does not need to.

## The system around it

The typeface is one part of a larger visual system, and you cannot really talk about Transport without talking about the system it belongs to.

Blue for motorways. Green for primary routes. White for minor roads. Brown for tourist attractions. Rectangles for direction, triangles for warnings, circles for mandatory instructions. Consistent chevrons, arrow shapes, and spacing. Calvert designed most of the warning symbols herself, including the silhouettes on the children-crossing sign, which she drew in 1965 and which are still in use. The little girl in that sign is based on a childhood photograph of Calvert herself.

If you learned to drive in the UK, this is the visual language you internalised before you could articulate what a visual language was. It is coherent the way a well-designed app is coherent, except the app is the size of a country and has been running continuously for over sixty years.

---

## Where else it went

Kinneir and Calvert's work did not stop at roads. They went on to design signage for British Rail, for Gatwick Airport, for the NHS. The family of typefaces that grew out of Transport shaped the public visual infrastructure of the United Kingdom for decades.

The Republic of Ireland adopted Transport with minor modifications. Parts of Australia, New Zealand, and Hong Kong adopted it too. If you are driving in much of the Commonwealth and the sign looks oddly familiar, that is why.

Transport is often confused with Johnston Sans, the Transport for London Underground typeface. They are cousins, not siblings. Johnston is older, more geometric, a different lineage entirely. Transport is the one on the roads.

---

## Getting it today

Transport was digitised multiple times over the years, often in semi-official cuts with names like Transport for the Road or Road Sign, of varying quality. The one worth knowing about is Margaret Calvert Sans, which Calvert released in 2019, at the age of eighty-three, as a cleaned-up commercial version of her original designs. Sixty years after designing the most-read typeface in British public life, Calvert sat down and made a modern licensable cut you can actually put in a design file.

She is, at time of writing, still alive, still working, still clear-eyed about what type design is for.

---

## Why I keep thinking about it

Calvert was twenty-two when she was hired. A woman in her twenties in 1957, trusted by a government department to design the typographic language of a national road network. I do not know how to say this without sounding sentimental, so I will just say it: that fact moves me.

And the mixed-case argument is the thing I keep returning to. A design decision, made on the basis of legibility research, that reduced driver error at motorway speeds. Not a metaphorical life-saver. An actual one. There is a lineage of design work that is about expression and beauty, and there is a lineage that is about quiet invisible correctness, and Transport is the best argument I know for the second kind.

Good design, in this sense, is design you never have to notice. You see the sign, you take the junction, you get home. The typeface that told you where to go disappears into the function it performed. The best compliment Transport ever receives is that millions of drivers have read millions of its letters and could not tell you what it is called.

I think about that every time I take the M1 north.
