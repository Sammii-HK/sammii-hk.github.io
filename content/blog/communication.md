Scroll [labs.sammii.dev/charts/communication](https://labs.sammii.dev/charts/communication/) and the year in the sticky bar counts up with you: 1440, 1450, 1465, 1495, 1500, 1516, 1532, 1540. Then the spine goes quiet. A label appears in the space, "100 years", and the next entry is 1640. A little further on there is another one, "60 years", before 1700.

Those gaps are to scale. Wherever two entries are more than forty years apart the spine opens up and says how long, so a century in which nothing on this chart happened looks like one. It is the detail that makes it read as history rather than a list.

## A student infographic, rebuilt

The original was an infographic I made as a student. The rebuild puts it on one spine, inventions on one side and type on the other, so the history of how we talk to each other and the history of how we set words down can be read against each other, and it keeps the writing. The footer says so plainly: the original writing kept, only typos corrected.

There are 65 entries, 1440 to 2024. Thirty-one are inventions. Nineteen are typefaces. Fifteen are what the chart calls type styles: movements and genres like Chancery Italic, Shaded Type, Fat Face and Stencil, which are not one face but a way of drawing letters that many faces shared.

Inventions sit on the left of the spine. Typefaces and type styles sit on the right. Each card has a kicker (the year and one of Invention, Typeface or Type style) and a title, and the text is clamped to two lines until you tap it.

## The controls

The bar that follows you down the page has three things in it. The year, which tracks whichever entry is nearest the middle of your screen. A row of century buttons, **1400s** to **2000s**, which smooth-scroll you to the first entry of that century. And a filter: **Both**, **Inventions** or **Type**.

The filter is more interesting than it sounds, because it changes the gaps. With **Both** on, the longest silence is the hundred years between Old-Face Italic (1540) and Transitional Roman (1640). Switch to **Inventions** and that disappears, because Royal Mail (1516) is followed by the Didot Point System (1775): a 259-year gap, the longest on the chart. Switch to **Type** and a different one opens between Clarendon (1845) and Akzidenz-Grotesk (1896).

## Where the histories touch

The pairings I like best are the ones where the two sides of the spine are obviously talking to each other.

The press and Blackletter share the first year, 1440. Moveable metal type made typography possible in the same moment it made the printed book possible, and the first printers drew their letters from the formal manuscript hands they were replacing.

The telegraph arrives in 1831, and around it on the type side are the first sans serif (Caslon's 1816 specimen), the Fat Faces and Old Style (both 1820), Reverse or Cameo (1828) and Ionic (1833). The same decades that compressed communication into wires were compressing letters into posters.

And the mobile phone, 1973, sits three years before Bell Centennial. The entry is one of my favourites. Linotype commissioned Matthew Carter in 1976 to update Bell Gothic for AT&T's phone books, which had to be legible at six point on rough directory paper. Carter drew it in four weights named for their jobs (Address, Name & Number, Bold Listing and Sub Caption) and cut ink traps into the joins, notches where strokes meet, so that the ink that runs into them completes the letter instead of obscuring it. It is a typeface designed for the failure of its own printing.

There are smaller pleasures. The Didot Point System entry gives the Didot point as 0.3759 mm. The Printing Press entry remembers the 1611 King James Bible that printed the seventh commandment without its "not". Some entries close with quotes from interviews: Brent Meheux, a graphic designer and educator, on the telephone and the computer; Chris Bigg, also a graphic designer and educator, on the photocopy and the fanzine; Calfyn ap Bryn, a broadcast producer, on families gathering round one television for the coronation in 1953; Karol Strykowski, a web and UX designer, on the web.

## Where the type column stops

Here is the thing the launch post did not say. The last entry on the type side of the spine is Bell Centennial, in 1976. After that the right-hand side is empty. Every one of the last fourteen entries is an invention: the internet in 1989, Google, Facebook, YouTube, Twitter, Instagram, Snapchat, Slack, WhatsApp, TikTok, Zoom, ChatGPT, Threads, and the Apple Vision Pro in 2024.

The chart's own introduction says the web arrived "with a hundred new faces a week". It does not have an entry for any of them. I think that is honest in its way: once type became software, and then web fonts, it stopped arriving as single landmarks you can put on a timeline. But it is also a gap in the chart, and if you want the digital half of the type story it lives in the [typography genome](/blog/typography), whose technology lane runs from PostScript in 1984 to variable fonts in 2016.

## What it does not do

Because the writing is the original, it carries the original's choices. The Didot entry calls Didot transitional; the genome calls it a Didone. The two charts disagree on dates too: Akzidenz-Grotesk is 1896 here (the entry says 1896 to 1899) and 1898 on the genome; Johnston Sans is 1918 here and 1916 there. The Newspaper entry is dated 1450, the press's consequence, while its own text says English newspapers began in the seventeenth century. Read the dates as the student's, and the entries as a voice rather than a reference.

The footer says the dates are the conventional ones: Gutenberg's press to 1440, the first sans serif to Caslon's 1816 specimen, the web to 1989. Spacing is to scale only where the gap is long enough to matter, and the gap is capped, so 259 years does not take up a whole screen.

The footer names no sources beyond that. If you want the type history with its sources named, the genome is the one to read.
